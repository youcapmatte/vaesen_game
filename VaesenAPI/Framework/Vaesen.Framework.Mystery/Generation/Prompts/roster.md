# Step: the people of the place

This Society is coming to this place for the first time. Write the people who live here, once: every later case here will meet the same people, so they must be ordinary, lasting and not built around any one mystery.

- For every place with an ownerRole, exactly one person who holds it: the keeper of a business (role keeper), the head of the household that owns a farm or manor (role owner), the vicar of a church (role clergy), the wise woman or cunning man of their cottage (role oldWays). Their occupation fits the ownerRole.
- The owner households of farms and manors: the head's spouse, one to three children or relatives, and a servant or farmhand where the place is big enough (role household, householdOf the head's id, relation such as "wife", "eldest son", "maid").
- The local official (a constable, the sheriff's man, or a crown bailiff for the district) and a schoolteacher if the place is big enough for a school (roles official and teacher); give them the place they live or work at.
- Three or four other residents (role resident): a carter, a widow who takes in washing, a miner's family, an old soldier. Each has one latentHook: something a later case might pull (a debt, a feud, a secret, a hope).
- Keep each short: age, gender, occupation, rank (one of: {{ranks}}), one line on who they are and what people say of them, one drive (what they want most in their ordinary life), and a few words of voice.
- Ids are npc- and a slug of the name, matching the name. Names fit the region and the 1870s. Swedish names of 1872: country people use patronymics. A son takes his father's first name and -son (Erik Ersson's son is Vilho Eriksson), a daughter -dotter (Karin Eriksdotter), and a wife keeps her own patronymic from her father, never her husband's (Erik's wife is Kaisa Jonsdotter). Family names (Lindqvist, Wester) belong to the gentry, townsfolk, soldiers and some families, and are inherited. Record a parent and child in relationships, so the name check knows they are kin.
- Names must be easy to tell apart by ear: no two share a first name or a first syllable, and no surname contains another person's first name, except a child's patronymic from the parent.
